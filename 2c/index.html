The pain it fixes

Arrival time is an input you do not control. When a ship slips, the morning turns into a puzzle: which guests can still make which departure, with the seats left, the all-aboard buffer, and parties kept together. Solved by phone at 6:30 a.m., it is easy to give away a seat that could have been kept, and it gets solved differently every time (my read). The re-planner solves it the same way every time and shows its work. Then the loss ledger adds up the season's refunds and staff time by cause: what Six Sigma calls the cost of poor quality, the number you need for pricing, staffing, insurance and advocacy.

How to use it

  1. Write down your timing rules. Departure times, tour minutes dock to dock for each product, walk-off and tender minutes, and how long before all-aboard you want guests back (a policy choice). Written down: the settings file, config/departures.json.
  2. Test the rules on last season. Use the feasibility checker below on three ships that ran late. Does the answer match what your team did? Written down: which departures each ship could make.
  3. Run the demo, then one of your own days. Export the day's manifest (no names), copy an example event file and change it, run python replan.py. Written down: the plan, the approval queue and the staff summary.
  4. A person decides. The duty lead reads the staff summary, confirms shuttle runs with the partner, fills the [FILL] gaps and approves each message. Written down: a name in approved_by for every message sent.
  5. Log every disruption. One row per event: date, cause, guests affected, rebooked, refilled, refunded, net lost, staff hours. Written down: the disruption log.
  6. At season end, run the ledger. python ledger.py gives the cost by cause and month and Number 3 with its uncertainty. Written down: the ledger summary and, if you share it, the aggregates-only advocacy export.

Feasibility checker: which departures can a cruise guest still make?

The same timing rule the re-planner uses, for one ship. A departure works when the guests can be at your meeting point before it leaves, and the tour brings them back at least the buffer before all-aboard.

Times and minutes only. No guest names or booking details.

The formula

Ready = arrival + minutes late + minutes to the meeting point. Back = departure + tour minutes + road delay. A departure works when ready is no later than the departure and all-aboard minus back is at least the buffer. The latest departure that keeps the buffer is all-aboard minus buffer minus tour minus road delay.

The full re-planner adds what one ship cannot show: seats left on each departure (the smaller of boat and shuttle seats), parties kept together, the notice you need before a departure, and which bookings to keep when seats run short.

How the plan is chosen

The goal, in order: keep the most net revenue on the day, then make the fewest moves, then put moved guests on earlier departures. A booking whose own departure still works keeps its seat Policy choice. The program runs a fast greedy method and an exact search, and says which plan it shows and whether it is proven best.

Greedy against exact, measured

On the fictional season, every ship running 45, 90 or 150 minutes late (1,108 cases), the greedy plan matched the exact plan every time. On 1,000 deliberately hard random cases with 8 to 14 parties and tight seats, the exact search kept more revenue in 101 cases: by 3.0% on average and by 19.7% at worst. When seats are cut on every shuttle run (the largest cases, up to 47 parties), the exact search hit its budget in 14 of 132 cases and says so; the greedy there was within 1.06% of the best plan found.

My read: on days like the fictional season the greedy alone would almost always do; the exact search is cheap insurance that proves it. These are fictional cases. The full table comes with the reference implementation, available on request.

My read on the fictional season: marine weather holds cost more than late ships for three reasons that multiply: more guests in total (205 against 180), fewer of them recovered (33.7% against 43.3%), and more net revenue per refunded guest ($156 against $124), because a held departure hits every sales channel while late-ship guests booked through the lower-net cruise desk. The gap in recovery rates between causes may be noise at these counts.

The advocacy export

advocacy_export.csv holds month by cause aggregates only: events, guests affected and recovered, net lost in whole dollars, staff hours. No booking references, no event IDs, no dates finer than a month, and a shared list of cause codes, so an association could add members' files together to show, for example, what road delays cost its members. Small cells can be combined, and dollars can be left out. Pooling needs a written data-sharing agreement Contractual

Prompt

Use ONLY the ledger summary pasted below. Do not add numbers, causes, benchmarks or industry figures that are not in it. Where the memo needs a fact the ledger does not have, write [FILL: what is missing] instead of guessing.

Write:
1. Two sentences: the season's disruption recovery rate (guests rebooked or refilled / guests affected) with its uncertainty range, and the partial cost of poor quality (refunds plus staff time), saying it leaves out goodwill, reviews and partner fees.
2. A short table of the causes in order of cost, with events, guests, recovery rate and cost.

5. A line on what the numbers cannot show (small counts per month are mostly noise; compare season to season).

Plain words, short sentences, no hype. Label any interpretation "my read". Start the memo with DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW.

How you will know it worked

Number 3 · Disruption recovery rate

Guests on transit- or weather-disrupted bookings who were rebooked or whose seat was refilled, divided by all guests on disrupted bookings. Count it from the disruption log monthly in season; judge it season to season.

Noise or a real change? The fictional season's 35.0% rests on 38 events. Resampling those events gives a 95% interval of about 28.3% to 41.5%. Next season will have its own interval; if the two overlap a lot, treat the change as noise. A month holds 6 to 9 events, so monthly swings say little.

Also watch: minutes from the first alert to the first approved message (note the alert time; the queue has an approved_at column), the share of touched net revenue the plan kept on the day, and staff hours per event. No targets: set them after a season of your own data.

Before you use it

The plan is a proposal. A person approves. Every message is a draft with [FILL] gaps. Guests may say no to a move, and the plan does not know their needs.

  • Legal mandate Seats per departure in the settings never above the passenger limit on the vessel's Coast Guard certificate of inspection (vessel-specific: confirm on your certificate).
  • Legal mandate Alaska's Personal Information Protection Act, AS 45.48, sets breach-notification duties for certain personal information. Confirm current text.
  • Recommendation Keep names, contact and payment data out of the manifest and the event files. The tools need none of it, and data you never export cannot leak.
  • Contractual Who may contact cruise guests about a change, and on what terms: check your cruise line agreement. OTA guests: check your OTA supplier terms.
  • Contractual Extra or changed shuttle runs: check your transport agreement (the fictional Kestrel Shuttle needs 30 minutes' notice for an extra run).
  • Policy choice The buffer before all-aboard, the notice before a move, whether a booking that still works keeps its seat, and your rebook and refund terms. The staff summary shows what the seat policy cost on the day.
  • Recommendation Weather and safety calls stay with the captain; the tool only re-seats guests. Ignoring this mixes a seating plan with a safety decision.
  • Recommendation Test on the fictional data, then on a copy of one past day of your own, before using it on a live morning. A wrong plan sent to guests costs more than the minutes it saves.
