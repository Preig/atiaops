The pain it fixes

Every season leaks money in three places: crew hired and trained twice, guests lost when a ship, a flight, the road or the weather breaks the plan, and capped slots left empty or sold below their value. Each leak is a process that did not work the first time. This audit is the Measure step of DMAIC: a dollar range for each leak, from your own numbers, so the first fix is chosen by size and not by the loudest complaint. Six Sigma calls the total the cost of poor quality.

Crew

20 hires

  • 22 seasonal positions, 6 returning staff (6 of 17 eligible: rehire rate 35.3%)
  • 4 early quits in the first 30 days (early-quit rate 20.0%)
  • About $2,800 per hire, all-in (estimate range $2,100 to $3,500)

Road

38 disruptions

  • 545 guests on disrupted bookings, 191 recovered (35.0%)
  • $51,596 net revenue refunded, 127.8 staff hours
  • Top cause: ship late or tendering from anchor (14 events)

Cap

79.1% used

  • 12,096 of 15,300 glacier slots used; at or near the cap (5 or fewer slots left) on 66 days
  • About 1,936 combo requests turned away: 206 at the cap, 1,730 for seats or timing, but 1,423 of those on capped days with only 65 slots left
  • 3,139 unused slots on the 87 days below the cap, mostly May, September and Saturdays

Fictional teaching business: Channel Light Excursions, Juneau. Season May 1 to September 30, 2026: 12,914 guests, net revenue about $1.97 million. All figures are invented for teaching.

How to use it

  1. Load the fictional example to see a finished audit. Write down which leak comes out on top and why.
  2. Type your own numbers over it, block by block. Leave a box blank if you do not know it; the audit uses what you have. Write the source of each number beside it on the worksheet.
  3. Set the uncertainty. Plus or minus 30% is the default. Widen it when a number is a guess. Write down your choice and why.
  4. Read "Start here". Start with the largest leak unless its range overlaps the next one. Write down the first tool you will open.
  5. Copy "Challenge my audit" into an AI tool. Write down every assumption it questions and the record that would check it.
  6. Save the audit and print the scorecard. Re-run it after next season and compare the two.

Numbers only. No names, emails, phone numbers or booking details: the audit never needs them. Everything stays in this browser.

1 · Crew leak

feeds Numbers 1 and 2

Crew leak = new hires × all-in cost per hire

From payroll or your hiring log: everyone hired for the season, including replacements for people who left.

Build the cost per hire from its parts

Average per hire. Skip a part you do not pay. Each part is an estimate.

The avoidable part: what if

A what-if, not a target. Try 10 points each and see how many hires that saves.

    How the crew leak is calculated

    Crew leak = new hires × all-in cost per hire. It counts every hire, not only the avoidable ones: some hiring is always needed. The what-if above estimates the avoidable part.

    Rehire rate (Number 1) = returning staff ÷ staff eligible to return. Early-quit rate (Number 2) = early quits in the first 30 days ÷ new hires.

    What if: extra returners = eligible × the points you enter (never more than the eligible people who did not return). Early quits avoided = the new hires still needed × the points you enter (never below an early-quit rate of zero). Hires avoided = extra returners + early quits avoided, assuming each early quit was replaced by one new hire. Dollars saved = hires avoided × cost per hire. Results are expected values, so they can be fractions of a person.

      2 · Road leak

      feeds Number 3

      Road leak = guests on disrupted bookings × average net per guest × (1 - recovery rate) + staff hours × loaded cost per hour

      From your booking system and disruption log: every booking a late ship, a flight, the road or a weather hold disrupted.

      How the road leak is calculated

      Road leak = (guests on disrupted bookings - guests recovered) × average net per guest + staff hours × loaded cost per hour. That is the same as guests × average net × (1 - recovery rate), plus staff time.

      Recovery rate (Number 3) = guests recovered ÷ guests on disrupted bookings. A guest counts as recovered when rebooked or when the seat was refilled.

      The average net per guest is an average, so it will not match your refund ledger exactly. In the fictional data, the ledger shows $51,596 refunded while the average-based estimate gives $53,996, about 5% apart. That gap is one reason the audit shows ranges.

      With your numbers:

        3 · Cap leak

        feeds Number 4

        Cap leak = (a) empty slots on days below the cap + (b) value gap on days at the cap + (c) seats-and-timing turn-aways on days below the cap

        From your permit use reports and booking system, split by day: "capped days" are days at or near the cap (for example, 5 or fewer slots left), and the rest are days below it. Three parts, three different constraints, shown separately.

        (a) Empty slots on days below the cap

        Volume you can add without a bigger permit: on these days demand binds, not the cap. Exploit it.

        (b) Value gap on days at the cap

        At the cap the lever is value per slot, not volume: price or product mix. The extra net per slot is your estimate; test it in 3.c before you rely on it.

        (c) Seats-and-timing turn-aways on days below the cap

        No departure had seats at a time that fit the guest's ship. Unlike the cap, this constraint is yours to elevate: add or move a departure.

        Season totals for Number 4 (optional)

        Not part of the leak. These give revenue per allocated slot (Number 4) and allocation used %.

        Identify which constraint actually binds, day by day, before you exploit it.

        In the fictional data, both constraints bound at once on the 66 days at or near the cap (5 or fewer slots left): 1,423 of the 1,730 seats-or-timing turn-aways fell on those days, when only 65 slots were left, so extra seats there would mostly have sold Whale Only ($117.58 average net), not combos. That is why part (c) counts only the 307 turn-aways on days below the cap.

        How the cap leak is calculated

        (a) = unused slots on days below the cap × value per filled slot × realistic fill share. Only a share of light-day slots could really be sold, so the fill share is your judgment; 30% is a starting point, not a finding. Revenue per allocated slot is a conservative value per filled slot: it sits below the average combo net and allows for light-day discounts.

        (b) = combo guests on capped days × extra net per slot. The extra net is what you believe premium pricing or product mix could add per slot on a capped day: your estimate, $10 by default. Requests turned away on capped days are the evidence that demand exceeded the slots; with none, treat (b) as a guess. It is value per slot, not more guests.

        (c) = seats-or-timing turn-aways on days below the cap × combo net per guest × recapture share. The recapture share is the part a new or moved departure would win back: your judgment, 25% by default. Turn-aways on capped days are left out, because there the allocation binds too.

        Revenue per allocated slot (Number 4) = net revenue of the capped product ÷ allocated slots. Also report allocation used % = slots used ÷ allocated slots.

        With your numbers:
