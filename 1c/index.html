Plan next season's crew from last season's data: headcount by week, who to recruit through which channel and by when, early quits and recruiting cost as ranges, and a workload check that ends in "schedule a check-in". It plans. A person decides.

The pain it fixes

Lean Six Sigma lens: capacity planning with an attrition curve. Most operators hire to the headcount they need on July 1 and discover the early-quit rate in June. Every early quit is rework: recruiting, travel, housing and training done twice. A staffing model treats attrition as a known input with a range, not a surprise: hires = new hires needed / (1 - early-quit rate), and each recruiting channel starts early enough for its lead time.

The fictional example: Channel Light Excursions, 2026

22seasonal positions
20new hires, including 4 replacements
20.0%early-quit rate: 4 of 20 left in the first 30 days
$56,000recruiting, at about $2,800 a hire ($42,000 to $70,000)

Six staff returned. The four replacements alone cost about $11,200 ($8,400 to $14,000) of rework. (Fictional numbers; cost per hire is the kit's fictional builder, about $2,800, range $2,100 to $3,500.)

My read: the early quits were predictable in kind, only the timing surprised. The four left 16 to 23 days after starting, which put all of them in the first eight days of the season, before any replacement could be recruited and trained.

How to use it

  1. Check the fit with the three questions below. Write down: this rung, or the lower rung to start with.
  2. Try the calculator with your own counts (two minutes, no code). Write down: the latest start-recruiting date for each channel.
  3. Run the demo of the Python model (five minutes, no keys, no internet). Write down: the three headline numbers: positions at peak, first deadline, cost range.
  4. Export last season: each hire's role, returning yes or no, start and end dates (no names), plus bookings or a forecast and next season's ship calls. Write down: how many new hires you had and how many left in the first 30 days.
  5. Set config.json: crew ratios (never below your vessel's certificate of inspection), shifts per week, buffer, lead times and costs by channel, your workload thresholds. Rerun until the model check reproduces last season's staffing. Write down: each ratio and who agreed to it.
  6. Run your plan and read the report. Write down: every start-recruiting date in your calendar, with an owner for each channel, and each check-in on the manager's calendar.
  7. After the season, add the new start and end dates and rerun. Write down: Numbers 1 and 2, season over season.

The formulas
  • Returning staff = eligible to return x rehire rate, rounded, never more than the positions.
  • New hires to open = positions - returning staff.
  • Hires in all = new hires / (1 - early-quit rate). Each early quit is replaced, and a replacement can quit too. With last season's 16 new hires and 20%, that gives 20, what the fictional business made.
  • Range of the rate: the Wilson 95% interval for last season's early quits out of new hires. With 4 of 20 it runs from 8.1% to 41.6%: one person is 5 points.
  • Latest start-recruiting date = season opens - onboarding days - lead time in weeks x 7. SOON means within 28 days of the plan date; LATE means already past it.
  • Cost = hires in all x cost per hire, plus or minus your percent. An estimate.

How the model works

The reference implementation, available on request, is a small Python program (standard library only) that does what the calculator cannot: it follows the season week by week.

  1. Demand. Last season's departures and guests per departure, moved to next season's weekdays, or your forecast. Ship calls per day from next season's port schedule.
  2. Shifts. Your ratios: crew per departure (more guests, more guides), dock staff per ship call, fixed roles per day.
  3. People. Shifts become people at your shifts per week, with a buffer. Positions are the busiest week's count, needed from the first week it is reached.
  4. Attrition curve. Who left in which week of tenure last season, with a Wilson interval and a bootstrap band, and a small-sample warning.
  5. Hiring calendar. Returning staff first, long-lead channels for the earliest positions, the latest start date for each.
  6. Simulation. 2,000 seeded seasons per scenario: how uncertain the rate is, plus luck. Hires and cost as P10 to P90, rehire 35% against 50% on paired seasons.
  7. Workload check. Hours and days off per person by role and week, last season's schedules by role, and a draft rota line by line. Output: "schedule a check-in".

P10 to P90 holds 8 of 10 simulated seasons. Budget with the range, not the middle.

My read: at a 10% buffer, no role gets an extra person at its peak, because 10% of a team of 3 to 6 is less than half a person. Peak slack is captain 0.2 of a person, deckhand 0.4 of a person, naturalist guide 0.4 of a person, dock coordinator 0.0 of a person, reservations 0.2 of a person. With so little slack, one early departure is enough to take away days off until a replacement works alone, which is what the WATCH items show. A buffer for a small team is a whole person or nothing (Policy choice).

How you will know it worked

    
Number 1 · Rehire rate

    
Staff who return this season / staff eligible to return from last season (finished in good standing). Count once, when the season opens. Want: up. It is the scenario lever in this model.

    
Number 2 · Early-quit rate

    
New hires who leave within their first 30 days / new hires who started. Count it 30 days after the last start of the season. Want: down.

    
Plan against actual

    
After the season, put the plan next to what happened: hires made against the P10 to P90 range, the date each channel actually started against its latest start date, and weeks a role ran below need. A season outside the range is a reason to recheck the ratios and lead times, not proof the model is wrong.

    
Noise or a real change? With 20 new hires, one early quit moves the rate by 5 points, and 4 of 20 has a 95% interval of 8.1% to 41.6%. A move from 20% to 15% is one person. (my read) Call it a change when it holds for two seasons, or when the intervals stop overlapping. No targets: set them after a season of your own data.

  


  

    
Before you use it

    
It plans. A person decides. Every report and memo is marked DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW. The model counts positions and dates; it never chooses, scores, ranks or flags a person.

    
    
      
  • Legal mandate Hiring decisions. Federal anti-discrimination law (Title VII, ADA, ADEA) applies to hiring decisions whatever tool helps make them. This model plans counts and dates; it does not pick people. Confirm current text.
    • 
      
  • Legal mandate Overtime. Alaska Statute AS 23.10.060 requires overtime at one and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 8 in a day or over 40 in a workweek, with exemptions in AS 23.10.055 and in AS 23.10.060 itself. The model flags planned shifts and weeks over those lines as reminders. Confirm the current text and whether any exemption applies with Alaska DOLWD Wage and Hour. Checked 11 Oct 2026.
    • 
      
  • Legal mandate Vessel crew. The minimum crew and the passenger limit for each vessel come from its Coast Guard certificate of inspection. Vessel-specific: your ratios in config.json can never go below your COI.
    • 
      
  • Contractual J-1 and H-2B. Obligations and timing vary by sponsor and program. Check your sponsor agreement and current DOL and State Department rules. The lead times here are fictional.
    • 
      
  • Contractual AI memo. If you use a real model for the optional memo, check the provider's API data terms first. The model sees aggregates only.
    • 
      
  • Policy choice Ratios, buffer, shifts per week, thresholds, channel mix, any returning bonus. Business decisions. Write down who set each one and when.
    • 
      
  • Recommendation No person scores, ever. The workload check reads schedules (hours, days without a day off), and its only output is "schedule a check-in", owned by a manager. Keep it that way: a score on a person becomes an input to hiring or discipline, and the federal laws above apply to those decisions.
    • 
      
  • Recommendation Privacy. Export IDs, roles, dates and counts only: never names, emails, phone numbers, IDs or medical details. The loader drops columns it does not use and says which. If you keep staff personal information on file, Alaska's Personal Information Protection Act (AS 45.48) sets breach-notification duties for certain personal information Legal mandate; confirm current text.
    • 
      
  • Recommendation Test on the fictional data first. Then on a copy of your own export. A wrong column caught on fictional data costs nothing; one caught after the plan is in the calendar costs a channel's lead time.
