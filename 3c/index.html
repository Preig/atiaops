Revenue-per-slot optimizer

At a permit cap, the number that grows the business is net revenue per allocated slot. This rung finds out which limit actually stops your guests, then tests prices and selling rules per day type against ranges, not point guesses. It never changes a price: it writes a memo for the owner.

  • Time 2 to 5 days with an analyst to fit your exports, then about an hour before each season (estimate)
  • Cost analyst time; the software is free; an optional AI memo adds model API fees (estimate)
  • Who owner plus an analyst or developer
  • Feeds Number 4

It tests. You decide. The optimizer and the calculator do arithmetic on counts and prices you supply. They change nothing in your booking system, and every report and memo is marked DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW. The examples use Channel Light Excursions, Juneau, a fictional teaching business. The pain it fixes

Lean Six Sigma lens: Theory of Constraints at a regulatory cap. The glacier allocation caps combo guests at 100 a day, and a regulatory cap cannot be raised by working harder. So first identify which limit actually stops guests, day by day: the allocation, or your own seats and departure times. Then exploit the cap: no empty slots, and the highest-value use of every slot. Then subordinate everything else (prices, the seat plan, which departures you offer to whom) to it. On a day the cap is full, more guests cannot raise revenue; only the value of each slot can. That is why, at a cap, pricing and mix become the growth lever, and revenue per allocated slot (Number 4) is the throughput metric.

The fictional example: Channel Light Excursions, 2026

$115.67revenue per allocated slot: $1,769,688 / 15,300 slots
79.1%of the allocation used (12,096 of 15,300)
66days at or near the cap (5 or fewer slots left; 33 with none)
1,936combo requests turned away: 206 at the cap, 1,730 for seats or timing

Both limits at once. 1,423 of the 1,730 seats-or-timing turnaways fell on the 66 days at or near the cap, when only 65 slots were left in total. A guest turned away for a seat on those days could not have had the glacier anyway, so extra seats there mostly sell Whale Only ($117.58 net per guest who travels) rather than the combo ($157.84). Only 307 seats-or-timing turnaways fell on the 87 below-cap days: that is where moving or adding a departure, a constraint the owner controls, wins back combo guests. (Fictional numbers.)

My read: the permit was not the main thing turning guests away; seats and ship timing were, and on the busiest days both ran out together. The lever that is left on those days is the value of each slot. Unused slots sit in May, September and on Saturdays, which is a fill problem for 3.a and 3.b, not a price problem.

How to use it

  1. Check the fit with the three questions below. Write down: this rung, or the lower rung to start with.
  2. Run the what-if with last season's numbers, then one change at a time. Write down: last season's revenue per slot and the plan's range.
  3. Read the sample report, or run the demo bundle (about five minutes). Write down: the binding constraint on heavy, medium and light days.
  4. Point the bundle at your own exports (counts and prices only, no names) and read the replay check first. Write down: whether the replay lands within a few percent of your files.
  5. Turn the report into a decision memo with the prompt below. Write down: the decision, who approves it and by when.
  6. Test before you switch: one price change on part of the inventory for a month. Write down: the test, the number you will watch and the result that makes you stop.
  7. Build the evidence pack before any permit renewal or conversation with the land manager or city. Write down: what your permit asks you to report, and by when.

      The arithmetic
      
Allocated slots = allocation per day x days. Last season per slot = share used x combo net per booked guest. Plan per slot = plan share used x combo net per booked guest x (1 + premium share x premium uplift). Change = plan minus last season = (plan share used minus last season's share) x net per guest, plus plan share used x net per guest x premium share x uplift. Range = last season + change x (1 minus uncertainty) to last season + change x (1 + uncertainty). Whale-only net = whale-only guests per day x days x whale-only net per guest. Seat check: allocation x plan share used + whale-only guests per day, against seats per day.

      
The "Load the fictional example" button uses the optimizer's recommended plan from the sample report: the plan's median share used, the share of combo guests on heavy days, and the heavy-day price change as the premium. The optimizer's own median is a little different because it also models seats, timing and whale-only prices.

    

  


  

    
How the optimizer works

    
A Python program (standard library only) that runs on the fictional data in about a minute or less and on your own exports after a few days of setup (estimates).

    
    
      
  1. Identify the constraint. From the daily log: requests blocked at the cap and blocked for seats or timing, by day type, and on days at or near the cap against below-cap days.
    2. 
      
  2. Day types. The kit's rule, shared with 3.b: heavy if port load is 70% or more or last season's same-weekday, same-month use was 95% or more; light only if port load is under 40% and use under 75%; medium otherwise.
    3. 
      
  3. Requests, not sales. Sold + turned away (both causes) + an estimate of guests who took Whale Only when the glacier was full. A fitted line on passengers in port and month gives requests per day.
    4. 
      
  4. A booking day, party by party. Cruise parties only fit departures that are back before their ship's all-aboard; each departure has 30 seats; the allocation caps the combo at 100 a day. It replays 2026 within a few percent.
    5. 
      
  5. 72 options per day type. 6 combo prices x 3 whale-only prices x 4 selling rules: as 2026, protect combo seats from whale-only, steer independent guests to the departures ships use least, or both.
    6. 
      
  6. 2,000 Monte Carlo draws. Seeded. Demand level, price response (elasticity -2.0 to -0.4, never measured) and whale-only acceptance drawn from ranges; every option sees the same draws.
    7. 
      
  7. Decision rule. Change a setting only if it beats the 2026 settings in 80 of 100 draws and each changed lever does so on its own (Policy choice). Plus a use-neutral alternative that never lowers allocation use.
    8. 
      
  8. Sensitivity and scenarios. One input at a time; and owner-controlled changes: a 60-minute return buffer, an added 11:45 departure on heavy or on medium days.
    9. 
    

    
Why the added departure on heavy days sells mostly Whale Only: the simulator stops selling the combo at the allocation whatever the seats, so on days at or near the cap extra seats can only take whale-only guests. The scenario table shows combo and whale-only guests separately, and it does not model the cost of a second vessel.

Decision memo prompt

      

You are helping the owner of [BUSINESS NAME] decide next season's prices and seat rules for a tour that is limited by a permit allocation. Below is a report from a revenue-per-slot model. Turn it into a one-page decision memo for the owner.

Rules:
1. Start with this exact line: DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW
2. Use only numbers that appear in the report. Do not calculate new totals or invent figures. If the owner needs a number the report does not have, write [FILL: what is missing].
3. Keep every range as a range (P10 to P90). Never present a median as a sure result.
4. Label every obligation: Legal mandate (cite it and add "confirm current text"), Contractual (name the agreement to check: permit, cruise line or OTA contract), Policy choice (the owner's call), or Recommendation (give the reason and the cost of ignoring it). Do not write "must" or "required" unless it is a cited legal mandate.
5. Do not decide or change any price. Present options. The owner decides.
6. No names, emails, phone numbers or booking references.

Structure:
- Decision needed by [DECISION DATE]. Approver: [OWNER OR MANAGER ROLE].
- The binding constraint in two sentences: which limit actually stopped guests, by day type.
- Two or three options. For each: what changes, revenue per allocated slot (range), season net revenue (range), allocation used, and the main risk.
- What to test first, how we will know it worked (which number, how often), and what result would make us stop.
- What to check before acting (permit, contracts), each with its label.
- Questions for the owner: every [FILL] and every open question in the report.

Keep it under 400 words. Plain language, short sentences, no hype.

Report:

How you will know it worked

    
Number 4 · Revenue per allocated slot

    
Net revenue of the capped product (the combo) / allocated slots for the season. Report allocation used % beside it, every time: a higher Number 4 bought with lower use is a trade-off the owner should see. Want: up.

    
How often: count both monthly in season, and compare season against season. Keep the daily log (allocation used, requests turned away at the cap, turned away for seats or timing) so next fall's run starts from your own numbers.

    
Noise or a real change? In the fictional model, day-to-day luck alone moved 2026 revenue per slot by 
about $4.66 per slot between the lowest and the highest of 10 replays ($113.28 to $117.94)
. A season-to-season change smaller than that is not evidence (my read). A price test on part of the inventory is cleaner: compare the tested days with similar untested days in the same month. No targets: set them after a season of your own data.

  


  

    
Before you use it

    
It tests. A person decides. Every report and memo is marked DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW. The tools never change a price, an inventory setting or a channel; a person does, after reading the ranges.

    
    
      
  • Contractual Your permit or agreement. Check what it says about actual-use reporting, how a visit is counted, and whether use is considered at renewal. Commercial-use terms vary; this page makes no claim about use-it-or-lose-it rules or any threshold.
    • 
      
  • Contractual Cruise line and OTA agreements. Check them before changing the rate a cruise line pays, the price on an OTA, or which departures a channel can sell (steering). The sample report shows the plan with cruise-line rates held at 2026.
    • 
      
  • Legal mandate Vessel passenger limits. Each vessel's passenger limit comes from its Coast Guard certificate of inspection, including any second vessel for an added departure. Vessel-specific: confirm the current certificate.
    • 
      
  • Legal mandate Overtime. Added departures add crew hours. Alaska overtime rules apply (AS 23.10.060, exemptions in AS 23.10.055); confirm the current text with Alaska DOLWD Wage and Hour.
    • 
      
  • Contractual AI memo. If you use a real model for the optional memo, check the provider's API data terms first. The memo path sends report.md only: aggregates, no bookings.
    • 
      
  • Policy choice Prices, selling rules, the return buffer, day-type thresholds and the decision rule. Business decisions. Write down who set each one and when.
    • 
      
  • Policy choice The return buffer. In the fictional 2026 bookings, about 1 in 5 cruise guests were on departures that returned less than 60 minutes before all-aboard, though the stated plan is 60 minutes. Decide the rule, with the guest-safety side in view, before you price seats.
    • 
      
  • Recommendation Test before you switch. Apply one price change to part of the inventory for a month and compare. Cost of skipping it: the price response, the biggest unknown in the model, stays unmeasured for another season.
    • 
      
  • Recommendation Privacy. Export counts, dates, times, prices and IDs only: never names, emails, phone numbers or payment data. The loader drops columns it does not use and reports their names only.
