The pain it fixes

Arrival time is an input you do not control. Ships run late or anchor and tender guests ashore, fog holds flights, roads back up, and the marine forecast turns. At a cruise port these are not rare accidents; they are a process with a rate. FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis) is the Six Sigma tool for exactly this: list how the guest's journey to your dock can fail, score each failure for severity, occurrence and detection, and spend your countermeasures where the risk is highest. Then write the standard response once, so the person on duty follows it instead of improvising.

    How to use it

    1. Book two hours with the owner, the person who runs the dock on a busy day, and one captain. Write down who was in the room.
    2. Score the journey FMEA (Section 1). Start from the fictional rows, change them to your route, add your own. Use your disruption log for occurrence. Write down the top five and every severity 9 or 10.
    3. Write a countermeasure and an owner role for each of those rows, with a date. Write them in the row.
    4. Answer the policy check (Section 2). Every gap is a decision someone makes before May. Write down who decides each gap and by when.
    5. Build the message pack (Section 3) for the scenarios on your action list. Paste each prompt into Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini, check every draft with the checker, and have the owner approve it. Save the approved texts as your standard messages.
    6. Build "Getting here, honestly" (Section 4). The owner approves it; then it goes on your website and in your booking confirmation. Write down the date it went live.
    7. After the season, re-score occurrence and detection from your log and write down the new action list.

    1. Journey FMEA builder

    One row per way the journey can fail, from the ship or flight to your boat. RPN (risk priority number) = Severity x Occurrence x Detection, each scored 1 to 10. The table ranks rows by RPN. Any row with severity 9 or 10 is flagged whatever its RPN, because a low RPN can hide a severe failure.

    Write failure modes, causes and roles. No guest names, staff names, booking details or phone numbers. Everything stays in this browser.

    How to score: severity, occurrence, detection

    Severity scores the worst credible effect on the guest and the business. Only a change to the process or design that removes the effect lowers it; better checks do not.

    Occurrence scores how often the cause or failure happens. Count it from your disruption log. A season here is about 150 operating days. These bands are a starting scale: adjust them with your team once, then keep them the same season to season.

    Detection scores how early and how surely your current controls catch it before the guest feels it. 1 is best. No control at all is 10.

    Five rules a Black Belt would check
    1. Score with the team, not alone. The person on the dock knows occurrence and detection better than the owner.
    2. Severity follows the effect, not the cause. Two rows with the same effect get the same severity.
    3. RPN sorts; it does not measure. It multiplies ordinal scores, so equal RPNs can hide very different risks: S10 x O2 x D2 = 40 and S2 x O5 x D4 = 40. That is why severity 9 or 10 always gets an action. The 2019 AIAG and VDA FMEA handbook replaces RPN with an "action priority" table that weighs severity first; this page keeps the classic RPN because it is simpler to run in a small team.
    4. No fixed RPN threshold. Work the top of the list and every severity 9 or 10, then move down as capacity allows.
    5. Close the loop. When an action is done, re-score. Better detection lowers D; fewer causes lower O; only removing the effect lowers S.

            How you will know it worked
            
    
              
      
            • Number 3, disruption recovery rate: guests on transit- or weather-disrupted bookings who were rebooked or whose seat was refilled, divided by all guests on disrupted bookings. Count it per season, by cause. Fictional 2026: 191 of 545 = 35.0%.
              • 
      
            • Minutes from first alert to first guest message: for each disruption, write down when you first knew (the alert) and when the first message went out after approval. Track the median per season against your first-notice target.
              • 
    
            
    
            Noise versus a real change. A season has a few dozen events, and guests on one event share an outcome. On the fictional 2026 log, resampling the 38 events gives a rough 95% range of 28% to 42% around the 35.0%. A formula that treats every guest as independent says 31% to 39%, which is too narrow. (Arithmetic on the fictional data; your range depends on your events.) My read Treat a season-to-season change smaller than about 7 points as possibly noise, and look at it by cause before you credit the playbook.

            Before you use it
            
    
            AI drafts. You approve. Every prompt on this page asks for DRAFT - PENDING OWNER REVIEW on the first line and [FILL] where a fact is missing. Nothing here sends, books, refunds or decides anything.
            
    
              
      
            • Recommendation Safety calls stay with a qualified person. The captain on duty (or another qualified person) makes every weather and vessel safety call. The FMEA, the policies and the messages never make it. Reason: a message drafted to keep a departure running can pressure a captain; the cost of that is counted in people, not refunds.
              • 
      
            • Legal mandate Vessel passenger limits come from each vessel's Coast Guard certificate of inspection. A rebook or a refilled seat never puts more guests on a departure than the certificate allows. The limit is vessel-specific: check your certificate.
              • 
      
            • Contractual Cruise-line guests. Who contacts the guest, which refund applies and what you may offer come from your cruise line contract. Check your contract before you write the cruise-line policy line.
              • 
      
            • Contractual OTA guests. Messaging outside the OTA and refund terms are set by your OTA supplier agreement. Check it.
              • 
      
            • Contractual Partner transfers. Who pays for an extra or changed shuttle run, and how much notice the shuttle needs, belong in your agreement with the shuttle partner. Check it.
              • 
      
            • Contractual Glacier or park allocation. Moving a guest between departures on the same day does not change the day's count; refilling a seat with a new combo guest does. Check your permit's terms on counts and reporting.
              • 
      
            • Policy choice The all-aboard buffer, the decision deadline, the waitlist rule, how long the shuttle waits, goodwill offers and the first-notice target. Each is your decision; the page only checks that you made it.
              • 
      
            • Recommendation Keep guest and staff data out of AI tools. Templates use [GUEST FIRST NAME] and [BOOKING REF]; real names are merged in your booking system. Check your AI tool's data-use setting.
              • 
      
            • Recommendation Scores are team judgments. Write where each occurrence score came from (your log, or a team estimate) so next season's re-score can be checked.
